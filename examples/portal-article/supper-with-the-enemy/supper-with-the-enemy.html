Supper with the Enemy

What the last book of the Iliad knows about the opposite of conflict

The Iliad announces its subject in its first word. In Robert Fagles’s translation:

Rage–Goddess, sing the rage of Peleus’ son Achilles, murderous, doomed, that cost the Achaeans countless losses, hurling down to the House of Death so many sturdy souls, great fighters’ souls, but made their bodies carrion, feasts for the dogs and birds, and the will of Zeus was moving towards its end. Begin, Muse, when the two first broke and clashed, Agamemnon lord of men and brilliant Achilles.1 (Homer 1990)

Those lines seem to promise a poem that ends when the rage wins. Simone Weil’s essay on the poem is called The Iliad, or the Poem of Force (Weil 1956), and the title is fair to most of it. But twenty-four books later Troy is still standing and the poem closes on a funeral. Just before the funeral it puts a supper between two men who have every reason to kill each other. This essay takes that ending as a claim about what the opposite of conflict is.

The quotations here are filed under a thesaurus of ideas, and its entry for conflict is worth a look. The opposite it records is neither peace nor victory. It is dialogue. The neighbours it lists for conflict include control, stress, and dysfunction; for dialogue, cooperation, engagement, and democracy. Rage sits beside anger, and no opposite is given for it at all.

Two for one

A quarrel is already a kind of conversation, because each side is answering the other. Tolstoy’s story “A Spark Neglected Burns the House” follows two peasant households, once close enough to lend each other a sieve or a pail, through a feud that begins over a single hen’s egg. An old grandfather, watching from his place on the oven, describes the rule his son has been living by:

You say one unkind word to me and I will reply with two. You will give me one slap in the face, and I will retaliate with two slaps. No, my son; Christ did not teach us foolish people to act in such a way.2 (Tolstoy 2022, “A Spark Neglected Burns the House”)

The neighbours spend six years taking each other to court, and the verdicts settle nothing. “The success of either in having the other punished only served to increase their rage against each other, until they were like mad dogs in their warfare” (Tolstoy 2022, “A Spark Neglected Burns the House”).3 That is the trouble with victory as a cure. A verdict is one more message in the same exchange, and the loser answers it.

What turns talk into this? Martin Luther King Jr., writing after he had toured Watts in the days following the 1965 riot, refused to treat the violence as noise:

A riot is at bottom the language of the unheard. It is the desperate, suicidal cry of one who is so fed up with the powerlessness of his cave existence that he asserts that he would rather be dead than ignored.4 (King 2010, chap. IV)

Hannah Arendt, in “On Violence,” defines the same act in passing as “violence—acting without argument or speech and without counting the consequences” (Arendt 1972, “On Violence,” pt. III).5 King hears violence as speech from people nobody listened to. Arendt sees it as the point where speech has been given up. On either account the same thing has gone missing: somebody stopped being heard, and blows went on saying what words had failed to.

Going to the other person

If that is right, the opposite of conflict has to involve hearing, and hearing has a cost. Thich Nhat Hanh is blunt about where the cost falls:

When we begin to see that our “enemy” is suffering, that is the beginning of insight. When we see in ourself the wish that the other person stop suffering, that is a sign of real love. But be careful. Sometimes we may think that we’re stronger than we actually are. To test our real strength, we try going to the other person to listen and talk to him or her.6 (Hanh 2011, chap. 7)

Compassion held at a distance is untested. The test is the walk, and the walk is the subject of the Iliad’s last book. Achilles has killed Hector, and each daybreak he drags the body round the tomb of his own dead friend. Hector’s father, the old king Priam, crosses the lines at night to ask for his son. In Samuel Butler’s prose translation:

King Priam entered without their seeing him, and going right up to Achilles he clasped his knees and kissed the dread murderous hands that had slain so many of his sons.7 (Homer 2021, Iliad bk. XXIV)

He asks Achilles to think of his own father, who is as old as Priam and far away. What follows is no negotiation.

Thus spoke Priam, and the heart of Achilles yearned as he bethought him of his father. He took the old man’s hand and moved him gently away. The two wept bitterly – Priam, as he lay at Achilles’ feet, weeping for Hector, and Achilles now for his father and now for Patroclus, till the house was filled with their lamentation.8 (Homer 2021, Iliad bk. XXIV)

Notice what the two men leave undone. They reach no agreement about the war. Neither apologises, and nobody forgives. They weep in the same room for different people, and each recognises the shape of the other’s grief. Then Achilles has the body washed and wrapped, lifts it onto the bier himself, and insists that the old man eat.

After the meal Homer gives them a moment that the earlier books have made almost unimaginable: “Priam, descendant of Dardanus, marvelled at the strength and beauty of Achilles for he was as a god to see, and Achilles marvelled at Priam as he listened to him and looked upon his noble presence” (Homer 2021, Iliad bk. XXIV).9 As he listened to him. Only then do they settle something practical. Priam asks for eleven days to mourn and bury his son, and Achilles gives his word to hold back the fighting that long.

What a supper can and cannot do

John Paul Lederach, writing for people who work on conflicts for a living, makes the claim for dialogue at exactly the strength the poem supports:

Dialogue is essential to justice and peace on both an interpersonal and a structural level. It is not the only mechanism, but it is an essential one.10 (Lederach 2015, chap. 3)

Before the next reply. Tolstoy’s grandfather and Homer’s king point to the same small experiment. In a quarrel that has started doubling, skip one turn of the exchange. Go to the other person instead of writing to them. Ask what the dispute has cost them, and say what it has cost you. Leave the settlement for another day, and eat something together if you can. Priam and Achilles agreed on almost nothing, and the meeting still counted.

The supper does not end the war. The truce is for a funeral, and Priam himself allows that on the twelfth day the fighting may begin again. Even inside the tent the peace is thin: Achilles has the body washed out of Priam’s sight, for fear that the father’s grief would provoke him into killing the old man. And the meeting happened only because one side carried the whole risk and the other chose to let him in. King’s passage is the reminder that people who have been shut out cannot be asked to supply the dialogue alone. Lederach’s second sentence is the careful one. Dialogue is essential, and it is not the only thing needed.

The poem that opens with two men falling out comes to rest with two men at a table and the war unfinished outside. Homer makes no larger claim for the supper than eleven days. He shows only that the rage, which killing Hector had not satisfied, stopped there for as long as one man listened to another.
