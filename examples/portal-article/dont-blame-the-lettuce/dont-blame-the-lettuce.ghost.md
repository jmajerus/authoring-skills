

### What we lose when the first question is "who did this?"

When something goes wrong between people, the first question is usually *who*. Thich Nhat Hanh pointed out that there is one place where nobody asks it:

<!-- quote 849 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="849" data-work-key="quote:743" data-quote-locator="chap. Lettuce" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:compassion urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:understanding" data-concepts="human-work:blame human-work:compassion human-work:understanding" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:compassion urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:understanding" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/blame https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/compassion https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/understanding">

> When you plant lettuce, you don’t blame the lettuce if it does not grow well. You look into the reasons why it is not doing well. It may need fertilizer, or more water, or less sun. You never blame the lettuce. Yet if we have problems with our friends or our family, we blame the other person. But if we know how to take care of others, they will grow well, just like the lettuce.[^hanh]
> (Hanh 2016, “Lettuce”)

</div>

A gardener standing over a wilted row asks *why*, and the answer is a list of conditions: soil, water, light. This essay is about what happens when we ask the gardener's question about people, and about how hard it is to keep asking it once a name is available.

## What blame buys

Blame is popular because it is the cheapest explanation on offer. It finds a cause in one step, and the cause is a person who can be corrected or removed. John Dyckman and Joseph Cutler, clinicians who saw its targets as patients, describe the bargain:

<!-- quote 786 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="786" data-citekey="dyckman2003scapegoats" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/72W4DN46" data-quote-locator="p. 11" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:scapegoating" data-concepts="human-work:scapegoating" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:scapegoating" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/scapegoating">

> There is almost always some truth to the accusations against a scapegoat, but many other sins are laid against their name that rightly belong elsewhere. Punishing or excluding the scapegoat serves to relieve the system of the need to examine the structural problems of the system and of all concerned to explore their own participation in the problem. The ability of scapegoating to provide simple apparent 'solutions' to complex problems is part of its power.[^dyckman]
> (Dyckman and Cutler 2003, 11)

</div>

The grain of truth matters. The person named usually did do something, which is why the explanation satisfies, and why nobody goes on to ask what else had to be true for the failure to happen.

<!-- quote 850 -->
The same trade is made at the scale of a country. Vincent Hendricks and Mads Vestergaard, writing about populist politics, observe that "Scapegoats and simplified explanations become replacements for the world’s complex and often less than transparent cause and effect chains" (Hendricks and Vestergaard 2018, chap. 5.6).[^hendricks] An office looking for the person who broke the release and an electorate looking for the group that ruined the country are making the same substitution of a culprit for a cause.

The quotations in this essay are filed under a thesaurus of the human side of work, and its entries are worth reading as a small argument. *Scapegoating* sits beneath *abuse* and *stigma*. *Blame* is related to *shame*, *punishment*, *rejection*, and *accountability*. And the concept recorded as the opposite of blame is not praise or innocence. It is *inner security*.

</div>


## What blame costs

The price is paid in attention. Jeffrey Miller, in *The Anxious Organization*, describes where people's effort goes once blame becomes the usual response to trouble:

<!-- quote 190 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="190" data-citekey="miller2002anxious" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/EQEK32VN" data-quote-locator="p. 145" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame" data-concepts="human-work:blame" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/blame">

> In organizations where anxiety is often expressed as blame, to avoid being blamed becomes a constant preoccupation. People attempt to preempt blame by sending each other memos recapitulating who did what and when. Their attention shifts from avoiding a potential problem to avoid *being blamed* for it.[^miller]
> (Miller 2002, 145)

</div>

The memos have become email threads with half the company copied, but the shift is the same one. Work that could have gone into preventing the problem goes into documenting that it was someone else's.

<!-- quote 534 -->
Blame also spreads. Nathanael Fast, summarizing his own research, reports: "Our findings showed that blame was contagious, but not among those who felt psychologically secure. So try to foster a chronic sense of inner security in order to reduce the chances that you'll lash out at others" (Fast 2010).[^fast] Seeing it done makes doing it more likely, except for people who feel safe. The thesaurus entry and the research agree on what stands opposite blame.

## The gardener's question at work

Some trades have turned the lettuce principle into procedure. Philip Crosby, writing for quality managers in manufacturing, put it this way:

<!-- quote 531 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="531" data-citekey="crosby1980quality" data-work-key="zotero:users/827407/XJPNPR8S" data-quote-locator="p. 75" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame" data-concepts="human-work:blame" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/blame">

> Objectivity comes with not placing the blame for problems on individuals. Aim the questions and probing at the job. The job is what failed, not the individual. It may be that the two are imperfectly matched and you have to change one or the other. Either way, the individual has the chance to improve another time, under different conditions.[^crosby]
> (Crosby 1980, 75)

</div>

Four decades later, the people who keep large software systems running arrived at the same rule and gave it a name, the blameless postmortem. Unai Huete Beloki lists it among the practices of site reliability engineering:

<!-- quote 851 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="851" data-work-key="quote:744" data-quote-locator="chap. 7" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:accountability urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:learning" data-concepts="human-work:accountability human-work:blame human-work:learning" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:accountability urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:learning" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/accountability https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/blame https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/learning">

> No “pointing fingers.” There is no one to blame; avoid the human natural tendency of looking for guilty engineers; it is assumed that staff acted with the best intentions possible (based on what they knew, skills, and abilities). Focus on the system, not the person. This is the most difficult change for an organization, but it has the biggest impact.[^beloki]
> (Beloki 2022, chap. 7)

</div>

The same chapter reproduces Ron Westrum's typology of organizational cultures, which sorts them by what a failure leads to: scapegoating in a power-oriented culture, justice in a rule-oriented one, inquiry in a performance-oriented one (Beloki 2022, chap. 7). And it gives the practice a grammar. A review should not ask who changed the database schema. It should ask what the reason for the change was.

**Three rewrites.** *Who approved this?* becomes *What did the approval step check, and what did it have no way to see?* *Why didn't you test it?* becomes *What made shipping untested the easy path that day?* *Whose fault is the delay?* becomes *Where did the work wait, and for what?*

</div>



<div class="note scope" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" typeof="Concept" resource="urn:iasp:concept:scope" data-iasp-delivery="note" data-iasp-modifiers="scope" data-iasp-concepts="urn:iasp:concept:note urn:iasp:concept:scope" data-iasp-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/note https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/iasp/slugs/scope" style="background-color: #fdf0e7; border-left: 4px solid #b45309; padding: 14px 18px; margin: 18px 0; border-radius: 0 6px 6px 0; font-family: Georgia, 'Times New Roman', serif; line-height: 1.5;">
<div style="display: inline-block; background-color: #b45309; color: #ffffff; font-size: 0.68em; font-weight: bold; text-transform: uppercase; letter-spacing: 0.1em; padding: 2px 9px; border-radius: 3px; margin-bottom: 4px; font-family: 'Helvetica Neue', Arial, sans-serif;">◎ SCOPE</div>

None of this means nobody answers for anything. Huete Beloki describes the blameless review as resting on honesty, learning, and accountability (2022, chap. 7), and Dyckman and Cutler grant that the accusation usually holds some truth. Accountability asks a person to give an account of what they saw and did, and then uses it. Blame stops listening once it has a name. Deliberate harm is another matter, and so is the powerful person who would like a "no blame" rule to cover their own conduct.

</div>


## Lowering the temperature

If blame is what anxiety looks like in a group, a rule against it will not hold for long. John Paul Lederach, one of the founders of conflict transformation as a field, states the real task:

<!-- quote 852 -->
<div class="iasp-quote" vocab="http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#" data-quote-id="852" data-work-key="quote:745" data-quote-locator="chap. 8" about="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:conflict urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:inner-security" data-concepts="human-work:blame human-work:conflict human-work:inner-security" data-concept-uris="urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:blame urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:conflict urn:quote-mcp:concept:human-work:inner-security" data-concept-terms="https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/blame https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/conflict https://vocabulary.majerus.us/api/schemes/human-work/slugs/inner-security">

> In the middle of conflict, when people are often filled with great fears and unknowns, the challenge is to lower the level of reactivity and blame, while at the same time increasing a capacity to express a clear sense of self and place.[^lederach]
> (Lederach 2015, chap. 8)

</div>

The sentence has two halves and needs both. Lowering blame without the second half produces silence, which is how a "blameless" culture turns into one where nothing can be said. People can describe what went wrong, including their own part in it, only when saying so does not put their place at risk.

Thich Nhat Hanh ends his story with a child who heard the lettuce talk and afterwards asked her mother to remember to water her. Her mother asked for the same in return (Hanh 2016, “Lettuce”). Each of them is the gardener, and each is also the lettuce.
